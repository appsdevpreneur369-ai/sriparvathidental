A quick, sharp twinge when you sip cold water, bite into ice cream or eat something sweet is one of the most common reasons people visit a dentist. It is usually treatable — once the cause is known.

## What makes teeth sensitive

Under the hard enamel is a layer called dentine, full of tiny tubes leading towards the nerve. When dentine is exposed, temperature and sugar reach the nerve more easily. That can happen because of:

- **Gum recession**, which exposes the root surface
- **Brushing too hard** or with a hard-bristled brush, wearing away enamel and gum
- **Acidic food and drinks** such as soft drinks, citrus and some juices
- **Grinding or clenching**, often at night
- **A cavity, a cracked tooth or a leaking filling** — which need treatment, not just toothpaste
- **Recent whitening or scaling**, where sensitivity usually fades within days

## What you can try at home

- Use a soft toothbrush and gentle, small circular strokes.
- Brush twice a day with a desensitising toothpaste and give it a few weeks to work.
- After acidic food or drink, rinse with water and wait about 30 minutes before brushing.
- Avoid very hot or very cold food while the teeth are sensitive.

## When to see the dentist

Book a check-up if the sensitivity is limited to one tooth, lasts more than a few seconds, wakes you at night, or does not improve after a few weeks of desensitising toothpaste. These can be signs of decay or a crack that needs proper treatment.

At the clinic we can find the cause and, depending on what we see, apply a protective varnish, seal exposed root surfaces, replace a leaking filling or treat the underlying problem.
